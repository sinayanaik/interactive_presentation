02 / Standardization

Put every column on the same ruler.

A network sees only bare numbers. It has no idea that 3 bedrooms is a lot, while 3 sqft would be tiny. Left alone, living area (thousands) would drown out bedrooms (single digits) just because its numbers are bigger.

So we translate every column into the same everyday question: how ordinary or unusual is this house? On the new scale, 0 means average, +1 clearly above average, −1 clearly below, and ±2 rare. Two moves get us there: ① − mean (subtract the column's average: slides everything so the average house sits at 0), then ② ÷ std (divide by the standard deviation: shrinks or stretches so a typical gap from the average counts as 1). Press play to watch.

mean = the average house std (standard deviation) = the typical distance of a house from the average

Before and after, side by side

Blue columns are the standardized values. Every blue column now has an average of 0 and a typical gap of 1.

Why bother?

Give each input the same weight (0.5) and add them up, the way a neuron does. With raw numbers, living area takes almost the whole total just because its numbers are big. After standardizing, each input counts according to how unusual the house is.

Keep the numbers. The averages and typical gaps are saved with the model, and every future house is translated with these same numbers (section 09).

05 / Computation graph

Break the calculation into tiny steps.

A neural network is one long calculation made of a few kinds of tiny operations: multiply, add, activate, subtract, square. Drawn as boxes and arrows, that calculation is a computation graph. Below is the path from one neuron of your last hidden layer all the way to the loss, with real numbers for the chosen house. Everything else in the network is lumped into rest.

Forward, values flow left → right. Backward, each operation needs only one simple local rule to pass the blame from its output to its inputs. Chaining those rules right → left gives every gradient. That is all backpropagation is.

boxes = numbers we start with (leaves)circles = operationsblack = value (forward)∂ = blame: how much the loss changes if this value goes up a tiny bit
06 / Backward pass

Follow the blame backwards, one layer at a time.

The forward pass gave a price that is off. The backward pass answers one question for every weight and bias: if I nudged this number up a little, would the error get bigger or smaller, and by how much? That answer is the gradient (g).

It starts at the output with the size and direction of the miss (called δ, "delta", the blame). Then it walks back through the arrows. Each arrow's gradient = δ of the neuron it points to × the value that travelled along it. Finally, every number takes a small step against its gradient. It is the computation graph's local rules (section 05), applied to every neuron at once.

07 / Training

Forward, loss, backward, update. Repeat.

Training is just sections 04 and 06 on repeat, for many houses. One iteration has four parts. Forward: each house in the batch flows through. Loss: the misses are squared and averaged. Backward: blame flows back, summed over the batch. Update: every weight and bias steps against its gradient. Use Step to go one phase at a time, Play to watch, or fast-forward.

Batch size 1 updates after every single house: fast but jumpy. All 10 averages the blame over every house first: one calm step per epoch. Real training uses batches of 32–256 rows, shuffled every epoch. Your Python script uses Adam, a smarter version of the same old − η × gradient rule that adapts the step size for each parameter.

08 / Check the fit

How far off is it, in dollars?

These scores update as the network trains. They compare its predictions with the actual prices of the 10 houses. To make them meaningful, compare with a lazy baseline that guesses the average price for every house.

MAEtypical miss

baseline MAEalways guess the mean

Honest caveat: the network learned from these same 10 houses, so this measures fit, not generalization. A model can memorize its training rows and still fail on new houses. That is why your Python script holds out a test set that never touches the weights.
